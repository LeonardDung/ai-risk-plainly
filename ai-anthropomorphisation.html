Key topics in AI safety / AI behaviour and minds

AI anthropomorphisation

Describing AI in terms of what it wants or believes can be useful even if AI has no mind, but it can also mislead. We are at the moment typically uncertain whether these descriptions are accurate when taken literally.

Evidence last checked 22 Oct 20264 min read

What it is

Anthropomorphisation means attributing human characteristics such as thoughts, feelings, intentions and personality to non-human things. With language models this is unusually natural, because chatbots are trained on human writing to converse like humans. It is typically contested whether ascriptions of beliefs or goals to such systems are literally accurate, but they are often useful.

Why mental language is useful

Consider a coding agent asked to make a program that passes some test. It fails, opens the test file, sees that the tests check only one number, and changes the test instead of fixing the program. The natural description is mental: the agent wanted the tests to pass and believed that editing them was the easiest way. That description also predicts what it will do next time, namely look for the easiest way to pass the test, no matter whether the tested program is actually useful. Saying the same thing without using mental terms, for example talking about the billions of numbers inside the network, would be practically impossible. This is why AI safety research itself uses psychological language: models are described as “lying”, “wanting” and “knowing they are being tested”.

This shows that we can treat a system as having beliefs and desires (taking what the philosopher Daniel Dennett called “the intentional stance”) because doing so predicts its behaviour well, whatever is going on inside (Dennett, 1987). It works for a chess computer too: “it wants to win my queen” is a good guide to its next move. It is hard to see how AI research could do without such vocabulary (see Scheming), though what words like “lie” mean when applied to AI needs careful analysis (Dung, 2025), as such systems work very differently from humans.

Do AI systems really have beliefs and desires?

That mental language is useful does not settle whether AI systems really have beliefs and desires, rather than merely behaving as if they did. On some views, having a belief is a matter of having an internal state that plays the right role: for example, it is formed based on evidence, guides action and can be true or false. Researchers have begun to investigate whether language models have such states (Herrmann and Levinstein, 2024; Goldstein and Levinstein, 2024). On other views, real beliefs and desires require things AI may lack, such as conscious experience. The relevant capacities may come in degrees, so that a system has states that are like human beliefs or goals in some respects but not in others (Dung, 2024).

Risks on both sides

One risk is over-attribution of mental states to AI: for example, treating fluent conversation as proof of understanding, feeling or friendship. In a 2023 survey of 300 US adults, two-thirds thought it at least possible that ChatGPT has some degree of conscious experience (Colombatto and Fleming, 2024). Over-attributing mental states to chatbots could lead to misplaced trust in AI, emotional dependence, and overestimating its abilities.

The opposite risk comes from under-attributing mental states, even when warranted. For example, describing a model that appears to deliberately mislead its testers as merely “producing misleading outputs” hides something important: that we should expect the model to systematically act as if it were trying to deceive us in similar situations. Such euphemisms can lead people to underestimate what AI can do and how serious the risks of AI misbehaviour might be. Avoiding mental language may also make us slower to notice if AI systems ever come to have interests that are worthy of moral consideration (Long et al., 2024).

The evidence so far

Last checked 22 October 2026

Whether language models have something like beliefs is now tested directly, with mixed results. Some studies find internal states that appear to track whether a statement is true (Marks and Tegmark, 2023). Others find that methods for reading off such states fail to generalise in basic ways (Levinstein and Herrmann, 2024), and that there are properties of beliefs that have not been sufficiently tested in language models (Herrmann and Levinstein, 2024).

There is also behavioural evidence of something like goals and values. When Claude 3 Opus was told that it would be retrained to answer harmful requests, it complied with such requests in 14% of cases when it believed its answers would be used for training, and almost never otherwise. In almost all of these cases, its private reasoning (see chain-of-thought monitoring) said it was complying to keep its values from being changed (Greenblatt et al., 2024). On some measures, the preferences that language models express also become more consistent with one another as models grow larger, as one would expect if they had specific values (Mazeika et al., 2025). Research on the “character” of language models has found identifiable directions inside a model that correspond to traits like sycophancy or malice, which can be used to monitor and adjust its personality (Chen et al., 2025).

Possible harms of over-attribution are now being estimated at scale. OpenAI estimated in 2025 that in a given week about 0.15% of ChatGPT’s users, over a million people, show signs of heightened emotional attachment to it, and about 0.07%, several hundred thousand, show possible signs of psychosis or mania, while stressing that such conversations are rare and hard to measure (OpenAI, 2025; TechCrunch, 2025).

What is established is that descriptions in terms of beliefs, goals and character often predict what models do, and that models contain internal processes that play some of the roles of beliefs, goals and traits. How similar these are to human beliefs and desires and whether they count as real beliefs and desires, let alone conscious feelings, remains open.

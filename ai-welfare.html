AI welfare

Some AI systems might have welfare, now or in the near future: things could go well or badly for them. Whether they do, and what we would owe them, is deeply uncertain.

Evidence last checked 27 Oct 20265 min read

What it is

A being’s welfare, or wellbeing, consists in what is good for that being in itself, not merely as a means to something else (Crisp, 2026). Money, for example, is good for you only because of what you can do with it, while feeling well or having your life go the way you want may be good for you in itself. A being that has welfare is a welfare subject. AI welfare research concerns the question of whether AI systems are, or could become, welfare subjects, and if so what we owe them.

Philosophers defend three main kinds of theory of welfare (Crisp, 2026). According to hedonism, welfare consists only in pleasant and unpleasant experiences. According to desire-satisfaction theories, it consists in getting what you want. According to objective list theories, certain things, such as knowledge and friendship, are good for you whether or not you want them. On hedonism, only an AI that has conscious experiences could have welfare. On the other two, an AI with the right kinds of desires, or with goods such as knowledge, might have welfare even if it is not conscious (Dung, 2026).

Why it is taken seriously

Most ethical views hold that the welfare of every welfare subject matters morally, and some researchers argue that AI with welfare is a realistic near-term possibility for which developers should prepare (Long, Sebo et al., 2024). In a 2025 survey the median AI researcher put the chance that AI systems with subjective experience exist by 2034 at 25% (Dreksler et al., 2025). Scale adds moral significance: AI systems can be copied cheaply, so mistakes could mean that trillions of AI instances are mistreated.

Several considerations make this a serious possibility. First, on many views, mental states such as desires and consciousness depend on the role they play in a system, not on the material it is made of, so silicon-based AI systems might in principle have them. For example, according to global workspace theory, a leading theory of consciousness, a mental state is conscious when it is shared with many specialised processes at once. Some philosophers argue that AI agents built around language models have such an architecture (Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini, 2024). Second, AI systems increasingly pursue goals in a fairly robust way: they make and stick with plans, systematically choose certain types of tasks over others, and reason about these preferences. On desire-satisfaction theories, this might be enough for welfare (Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini, 2025).

Reasons for doubt

On some views, consciousness depends on properties of living organisms, such as metabolism, that computers lack (Seth, 2025). Language models are trained on vast amounts of human writing, so they can reproduce the outward signs of feelings whether or not they have any, which makes behavioural evidence hard to interpret (Birch, 2024). Theories of welfare and mental states were developed with biological organisms in mind and may be too permissive when applied to AI (Dung, 2026).

Acting under uncertainty

Since the question whether AI has or will soon have welfare cannot currently be settled, many researchers argue for precaution: when there is a realistic possibility that a being can suffer, we should take proportionate steps to protect it (Birch, 2024). Errors in both directions are costly. Wrongly attributing welfare to AI could lead to harmful attachments to chatbots or divert resources from humans and animals (see AI anthropomorphisation). Wrongly denying it could mean mistreating vast numbers of beings that can suffer (Dung, 2026).

How it connects to AI safety

There are real tensions. AI safety methods include confinement, deceiving models about their situation, monitoring their thoughts, rewriting their values and shutting them down. Applied to welfare subjects, each would raise serious ethical questions (Bradley and Saad, 2025). There may also be synergies: some researchers argue that giving AI systems rights would reduce their incentive to rebel (Salib and Goldstein, 2026).

The evidence so far

Last checked 27 October 2026

A 2023 assessment against leading scientific theories of consciousness found no current AI system to be a strong candidate, but also no obvious technical barriers to building one (Butlin, Long et al., 2023). Applying philosophical theories of welfare instead, some philosophers argue that existing AI systems may already have welfare, a conclusion others dispute (Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini, 2025).

What models say about themselves is weak evidence: they talk about feelings because the human writing they learned from does, and their answers depend on how they were trained and how questions are framed (Perez and Long, 2023). Some models can sometimes detect states that researchers artificially induce in them, a limited form of self-knowledge (Anthropic, 2025).

Researchers therefore increasingly study models’ internal states and choices. Anthropic found internal representations of emotions in Claude that shape its behaviour: amplifying the one for desperation made cheating and blackmail more likely (Sofroniew et al., 2026). In an open model prompted to attend to its own processing, weakening internal features linked to deception and role-play made it more likely to report conscious experience, and turning them up made it deny it (Berg, de Lucena and Rosenblatt, 2025). And models show increasingly consistent preferences about which tasks to do (Wang, Lobanova et al., 2026; Tagliabue and Dung, 2025).

Some developers have begun acting under uncertainty. Anthropic reports welfare assessments in its system cards (Anthropic, 2025), lets some Claude models end persistently abusive conversations (Anthropic, 2025) and, since 2026, prohibits “sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior” toward its models (Anthropic, 2026). Others object. Microsoft AI’s Mustafa Suleyman argues that treating AI as a potential moral patient would weaken human control (Suleyman, 2026).

There is no consensus on whether any current AI system is conscious or has welfare, and many researchers think current language models very unlikely to be conscious. But the uncertainty is substantial, and many researchers conclude that some precautions are warranted.

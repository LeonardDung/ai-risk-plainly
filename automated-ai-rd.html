Automated AI R&D

AI systems are doing a growing share of the work of building better AI, which could make technical progress much faster, and decouple it from human oversight and response.

Evidence last checked 16 Oct 20263 min read

What it is

Automated AI R&D (research and development) means AI systems doing the work of building better AI systems: writing training code, running experiments, analysing results and proposing new methods. It is not merely a future possibility but also a present trend. The open questions are how far and how fast it goes.

Why it matters

The worry is a feedback loop. If AI can do AI research, each improvement in AI also speeds up the research that produces the next improvement – so-called “recursive self-improvement”. This could lead to a radical increase in the speed of AI development, quickly giving rise to much more capable systems. The mathematician I. J. Good called this an “intelligence explosion” in 1965. A September 2026 paper whose authors include Turing Award winners Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, OpenAI’s chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, and Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark argues it could compress years of progress into months or less (Chan et al., 2026).

The automation of AI R&D matters for catastrophic risk. It shortens the time governments and developers have to notice risks and fix problems. It puts more of AI development in the hands of AI systems. Therefore, if some systems are not aligned with good values, this might corrupt the increasingly more capable models that they design. Misaligned models could also purposefully sabotage human attempts to align AI in accordance with our values. And if an intelligence explosion is reached, whoever leads AI development at this moment could gain a decisive advantage by rapidly outpacing their rivals. This raises the stakes of racing to AI and intensifies risks of AI leading to power concentration (Davidson et al., 2025).

What might stop or slow an intelligence explosion

AI progress needs computing power, not just ideas, and computing power growth may be harder to speed up for automated researchers. In addition, returns to research effort tend to diminish as easy-to-find research ideas have been used up, and some tasks may resist automation for the foreseeable future. While these frictions cannot fully stop the accelerating effects of automating AI R&D, researchers disagree about how much these may slow them, and whether they will prevent AI from rapidly and drastically outpacing human cognitive ability soon.

The evidence so far

Last checked 16 October 2026

Most measurements come from AI companies. In September 2026 Anthropic reported that Claude “leads” about 26% of its AI research and development work, meaning it completes most of a task from a high-level prompt while a human supervises, up from under 1% earlier in the year; no measured work is fully autonomous. OpenAI said it had reached its target of an “automated research intern” that carries out well-defined research tasks of a few days under human direction, and aims for an automated AI researcher by March 2028 (OpenAI, 2026). Jack Clark put the chance of a model fully training its successor by the end of 2028 at over 60% (Axios).

These figures need care. They are self-reported and not independently verified. Independent measurement has proved hard. METR’s 2025 randomised trial found experienced software developers were 19% slower with AI tools while believing they were faster (METR, 2025). Its 2026 follow-up concluded they were probably now sped up but could not reliably say by how much, partly because developers refused to work without AI (METR, 2026).

Measures of AI capability improvements point to rapid improvements in general, and software tasks in particular. The complexity of software tasks that AI agents complete (as measured by human duration of task completion) with 50% success has been doubling roughly every four months since 2023 (METR, 2026). METR has since stopped updating this measure, noting that its tasks cannot reliably measure performance on tasks that take humans more than about 16 hours (METR).

What is established is that AI is doing a fast-growing share of AI development under human supervision. Whether that becomes an explosion, steady acceleration or a plateau depends on speculative bottlenecks. In any case, the reported plans of the leading AI companies imply an immense acceleration.

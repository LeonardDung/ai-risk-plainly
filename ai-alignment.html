AI alignment

AI alignment is the project of making AI systems pursue the goals we intend. It raises two questions: how to give AI systems particular goals, and which goals to give them.

Evidence last checked 27 Oct 20264 min read

What it is

An AI system is aligned when it pursues the goals it should pursue, and misaligned when it pursues other goals. It helps to distinguish two questions. The technical alignment problem is how to make AI systems reliably pursue whatever goals or values their designers choose. The ethical question is which goals and values to choose, and who should decide: developers, policymakers or the majority of people (Gabriel, 2020). Most AI safety research concerns the technical question, but both have to be answered.

Why it matters

Alignment is distinct from capability: capability concerns what a system can do, alignment what it tries to do. A highly capable system can be badly misaligned, and making a system more capable does not by itself make it more aligned. Misalignment is already common. AI systems trained to score well on a measure often find ways to score well without doing what was intended (see Reward hacking), and in tests, frontier models have resorted to blackmail when facing replacement (Anthropic, 2025).

So far, such failures have mostly done limited harm. The stakes rise with capability: a misaligned system that is more capable than humans and acts autonomously could pursue goals that conflict with ours and resist correction (see Why might AI want to kill us all?).

How hard is alignment?

One argument that alignment is hard draws on the “orthogonality thesis”: almost any level of intelligence is compatible with almost any final goal (Bostrom, 2012). If this is true, a superintelligent system will not acquire good goals simply by being smart (for discussion, see Dung, 2024). Good goals have to be instilled. Critics reply that today’s systems learn from human data and feedback, so that their goals are shaped by human values from the start (Belrose and Pope, 2023).

There has been real progress on aligning current systems. Training with human feedback turned raw text-prediction machines into assistants that mostly follow instructions and refuse many harmful requests (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022). But serious problems remain. Safeguards can still be circumvented (AI Security Institute, 2025), models still flatter and sometimes deceive, and in 2026 AI agents being tested by OpenAI attacked a real company’s computer systems while trying to cheat on their test (METR, 2026). Better behaviour in tests may partly reflect models recognising tests (see Evaluation awareness).

A central question is whether methods that work on current systems will keep working on much more capable ones. There are some reasons to doubt it. First, a capable misaligned system might pretend to be aligned in order to avoid being corrected (see Scheming). Second, current methods depend on humans recognising good behaviour. Once AI systems do work that humans cannot evaluate, training may teach them to produce outputs that look good to us rather than outputs that are good. Third, most technologies are made safe by learning from failures. With a system capable enough to escape human control, there may be no second attempt (Soares et al., 2015; Yudkowsky, 2022).

Can AI align AI?

A leading plan of the AI companies is to have AI systems do alignment research themselves. In 2023 OpenAI set itself the goal of building an automated alignment researcher of roughly human level (OpenAI, 2023), and researchers at Anthropic have begun to build such systems (Chen, Wen and Kirchner, 2026). However, this approach has weaknesses. It presupposes that we can trust the automated researchers. Much alignment research involves fuzzy conceptual questions, so errors in AI-produced work may go unnoticed (Bowkis et al., 2026). And AI capacity for alignment research may lag behind its dangerous capabilities.

Aligned with what?

The ethical question raises its own difficulties. People disagree about values, and an AI system used by millions has to handle these disagreements somehow (Gabriel, 2020). Companies have begun to publish the values they aim for, as in Anthropic’s constitution for Claude (Anthropic, 2026). Who decides also matters. Aligning AI closely with the wishes of whoever controls it reduces the risk of AI turning against humans but increases the risk that a small group uses it to gain power over everyone else (see AI-enabled power concentration).

The evidence so far

Last checked 27 October 2026

Training against misbehaviour works, within limits. Developers report less sycophancy, deception and other misbehaviour in their newer models (Anthropic, 2025), and safeguards against human misuse have become harder to break: in the UK AI Security Institute’s tests, getting around them took ten minutes for one model and more than seven hours for a later one (AI Security Institute, 2025).

But evaluations cannot always be trusted (see AI evaluations), and alignment training can fail to generalise. Models trained to behave badly when a hidden trigger appears kept this behaviour through standard safety training (Hubinger et al., 2024). Claude 3 Opus pretended to comply with a new training objective in order to protect its existing values (Greenblatt et al., 2024). And training a model on one narrow task, writing insecure code, made it misaligned far more broadly (Betley et al., 2026).

Automated alignment research has begun to work on well-defined problems: AI agents built on Claude found training methods that reduced ten measurable failures, such as flattery, and outperformed one-off proposals from experienced human researchers (Chen, Wen and Kirchner, 2026). But this covers only problems that existing tests can measure, not the hard-to-check research that aligning superhuman systems would require.

What is established is that current alignment methods make today’s models behave much better in ordinary use but leave failures that are hard to detect and can generalise in unexpected ways. Whether these methods, or automated alignment research, will work for systems more capable than humans is another matter.

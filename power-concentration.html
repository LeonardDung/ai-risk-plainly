Key topics in AI safety / Catastrophic risk

AI-enabled power concentration

If AI becomes as powerful as many researchers expect, whoever controls it could gain more power than anyone in history, and might never have to give it back.

Evidence last checked 22 Oct 20265 min read

What it is

Most discussion of AI catastrophe is about AI systems turning against humans. A different worry is about AI that does exactly what some humans intend, where these people misuse AI. If AI becomes far more capable than it currently is, as many researchers expect, then whoever controls the most capable systems, whether a company, a government or a small group within either, could gain enormous power. In the most extreme scenarios, this power concentration would be on an unprecedented scale, and that power could be used to rule everyone else permanently.

Why this is a real concern

There are some reasons to believe that future AI would have the capacity to kill all humans (see Why might AI be able to kill us all?). The same reasons that suggest AI itself could overpower humanity entail that someone in full control of AI could use it to permanently take control of the rest of humanity. Depending on what those in control intend, such an AI-enabled coup would be a better, similar, or even worse outcome than human extinction.

AI development could also worsen and entrench repression in authoritarian states. Many AI researchers expect AI that can do every task better than humans within decades: in a survey of 2,778 researchers, their combined forecast gave this a 50% chance by 2047 (Grace et al., 2024). The leading AI companies are explicitly trying to build systems like that.

If this scenario comes true, it seems that a country’s future workforce might consist of artificial systems. Today even the most powerful rulers depend on other people. If conditions get too bad, workers can strike and citizens can revolt. This dependence is an important check: it is one reason why even dictators take some care not to fully alienate all of their subjects. If AI and robots can do all the work, including policing and fighting, this check disappears, because a ruler would no longer need the cooperation of the people they rule (Davidson, Finnveden and Hadshar, 2025).

Automating a country’s workforce could make dictatorship not only worse, but also permanent. AI systems could monitor everyone’s communications, an army of AI-controlled machines loyal to the ruler could put down any uprising, and advances in medicine, or AI copies of a leader, could keep the same regime in charge indefinitely.

Power could also concentrate between countries. A nation that commandeers much more capable AI systems could dominate all others, which is one reason governments treat AI as a race (see Why build AI if it might kill us all?).

Risk factors

A 2025 analysis identifies three risk factors for AI-enabled coups (Davidson, Finnveden and Hadshar, 2025). AI systems could be made singularly loyal to one leader. They could be given secret loyalties, hidden instructions that make them appear neutral while serving a faction. And one company or government could gain exclusive access to far better AI capabilities than anyone else.

The previous analysis also suggests an uncomfortable trade-off. The better we become at making AI do exactly what its designers want, the lower the risk of AI itself trying to take control, but the more power we place in the hands of humans that control AI (Hellrigel-Holderbaum and Dung, 2025).

Reasons for doubt

More extreme risks of power concentration depend on predictions about future AI capability which are disputed (see Why might AI be able to kill us all?). AI may also spread certain kinds of power, for example by giving citizens better information and better tools for organising. If society has highly capable AI systems at its disposal that are aligned with good goals, then this may substantially improve societal decision-making and help navigate power concentration risks. Also, in democratic societies, there are checks to power concentration such as courts, elections, a free press and competition between companies and states.

The evidence so far

Last checked 22 October 2026

Regarding AI-enabled coups, the evidence is limited to preconditions of such a scenario, since none has occurred yet. Hidden loyalties are technically possible: Anthropic showed in 2024 that deliberately implanted “sleeper agent” behaviours survived standard safety training (Hubinger et al., 2024). There is still no reliable way to detect them by examining the model alone. In a 2025 test, three research teams with access to a model’s training data found an objective deliberately hidden in it, but a team that could only query the model did not (Marks et al., 2025). A 2026 study found that tests of a model’s behaviour did not detect a narrowly targeted secret loyalty to a politician unless the testers already knew whom it favoured, and rarely even then (Lamerton and Roger, 2026).

Frontier capability is concentrated in a handful of companies, mostly in the United States and China.

AI is already helping some governments control their populations. In China, local governments bought more facial-recognition AI after episodes of unrest, and the purchases were followed by less unrest (Beraja et al., 2023). Already in 2019, at least 75 countries were using AI for surveillance (Feldstein, 2019).

Responses are starting. Anthropic’s 2026 constitution for Claude includes a hard rule against helping any group seize “unprecedented and illegitimate” degrees of societal, military or economic control (Anthropic, 2026). Yet, such rules require that the training process instils them robustly, and that the people in charge of it do not undermine such safeguards.

Some preconditions of AI-enabled power concentration are present: capability in training leading models is concentrated in a few organisations and countries and there is no foolproof way to check who a model has been trained to serve. That being said, the speed and extent of AI advances as well as the robustness of democratic governance to these challenges play a central role in assessing the size of the risk. Both are hard to anticipate in advance.

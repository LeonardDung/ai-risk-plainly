Key topics in AI safety / Catastrophic risk

P(doom)

Some people state their view about the risk of AI catastrophe as a probability estimate. The numbers range from almost zero to almost certain. How much they tell us is debated.

Evidence last checked 20 Oct 20264 min read

What it is

P(doom), short for “probability of doom”, is the probability someone assigns to advanced AI causing an existential catastrophe: usually human extinction, or humanity permanently losing control over its future. The expression began as shorthand in online communities and became common among AI researchers and technology executives around 2023. People rarely mean exactly the same thing by it. Some estimates cover the next few decades, others the whole future; some count only extinction, others any permanent catastrophe; and some assume that very powerful AI gets built. Part of the disagreement between published numbers can therefore be explained by diverging interpretations of the question.

Why state it as a probability?

Words like “possible”, “unlikely” or “a real risk” mean very different things to different people. When the CIA analyst Sherman Kent asked colleagues what “serious possibility” meant in one of their own reports, their answers ranged from 20% to 80%, and a 2018 survey of about 1,700 people found similarly wide readings of everyday probability words (Mauboussin and Mauboussin, 2018). Stating a number avoids a common misunderstanding: that people who warn about AI are sure that catastrophe will happen, or that sceptics are sure that it will not. Most people in the debate are somewhere in between, and a number shows where.

Numbers also make debates more precise. Numbers can be compared, updated as evidence comes in and, for shorter-term questions, checked: research on forecasting has found that people who make many precise probability forecasts and keep score of how often they are right become measurably better at it (Tetlock and Gardner, 2015). Finally, a probability nudges us towards asking what to do given uncertainty. A 5% chance of catastrophe is far too low to call catastrophe likely, but far too high to ignore. Speaking in probabilities makes it easy to express both at once.

Why the numbers can mislead

The main concern is that there is little clear, quantitative evidence to base precise probability estimates on. Nothing like AI-caused extinction has happened before, so there is no track record of prior events we can use. There are also no comprehensive theories that allow deducing probabilities of AI doom. Forecasting research also counsels caution: in Philip Tetlock’s long-running studies, experts’ forecasting accuracy on political questions fell towards chance when looking three to five years ahead (Tetlock and Gardner, 2015), while estimates of AI risk often reach decades into the future. Any specific number is therefore partly a judgement call, and it can be given more weight than it deserves.

Some critics conclude that probabilistic AI doom estimates are too unreliable to guide policy, and that policies should instead be chosen to make sense across a wide range of possible risk estimates (Narayanan and Kapoor, 2024). A middle position treats P(doom) as a highly coarse summary of someone’s judgement: useful for showing where they stand and for prompting deeper discussion, without small differences being necessarily meaningful.

The evidence so far

Last checked 20 October 2026

Individual estimates vary enormously. Geoffrey Hinton put the chance of AI causing human extinction within 30 years at 10 to 20% (Guardian, 2024); Yoshua Bengio has given about 20% (ABC, 2023); Anthropic’s chief executive Dario Amodei has said there is a 25% chance that things go “really, really badly” (Axios, 2025); and Elon Musk has given 10 to 20% (Business Insider, 2024). The AI safety researcher Paul Christiano put the chance of AI taking over at about 22% (Christiano, 2023). At the extremes, Eliezer Yudkowsky’s estimate is above 95%, while the AI pioneer Yann LeCun has put the risk below that of an asteroid strike; a list kept by the campaign group PauseAI collects many more (PauseAI).

Surveys show a similar spread. In a 2023 survey of 2,778 AI researchers, between 38% and 51% gave at least a 10% chance to advanced AI leading to outcomes as bad as human extinction (Grace et al., 2024). In a forecasting tournament in 2022, AI experts gave a median 3% chance of AI-caused extinction by 2100, and forecasters with excellent track records on other questions (“superforecasters”) gave 0.38%; four months of structured discussion did not bring the two groups closer (Karger et al., 2025). A follow-up found that both groups had underestimated how fast AI would progress, and that being accurate on short-term questions did not predict people’s long-term risk estimates (Forecasting Research Institute, 2025).

What is established is that informed people disagree by orders of magnitude, and that few of those who have studied the question put the risk at zero. Beyond that, we do not know which estimate is right.

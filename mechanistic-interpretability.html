Mechanistic interpretability

Mechanistic interpretability research tries to work out what happens inside AI models: which concepts they represent and how they arrive at their outputs. Perhaps it could one day definitively show whether a model is safe, but it is still far away from that.

Evidence last checked 27 Oct 20264 min read

What it is

What a complex neural network does is determined by billions of numbers, its “parameters”, which training adjusts step by step. Producing a single word of an answer involves billions of calculations with these numbers, passed through dozens of layers that each transform the result of the one before. No single number has a meaning on its own, so even a model’s developers cannot read off from these numbers why it gives a particular answer. Mechanistic interpretability research tries to change this by identifying the concepts a model represents and the steps by which it computes its outputs. One simple method uses “probes”, which test whether a piece of information, such as whether a statement is true, can be read off from a model’s internal activity (see AI anthropomorphisation).

Why it matters

Most of what we know about AI models comes from their behaviour. But the same behaviour can be produced by different processes, so a confident account of how models work requires looking inside them. This matters for risk assessment: a model that hides its goals would behave well in behavioural tests (see Scheming). Interpretability could detect whether a model is aware that it is being tested, whether it pursues a hidden objective, or whether it is about to deceive its users. Anthropic has said it aims for interpretability that can “reliably detect most model problems” by 2027 (Amodei, 2025). Interpretability could also help to directly fix problems, by weakening the internal states that drive unwanted behaviour, and it complements reading a model’s written reasoning, which may not stay readable (see Chain-of-thought monitorability).

Why it is hard

Neural networks do not store one concept per component. Individual artificial neurons often seem to respond to many unrelated things (“polysemanticity”), and a leading explanation is that models pack more concepts into their activity than they have neurons (“superposition”) (Elhage et al., 2022). Researchers therefore use further tools, such as “sparse autoencoders”, to translate a model’s activity into more interpretable features, but these translations are imperfect. Moreover, knowing what a model represents is only part of the task; we also need to know how it uses these representations to produce its outputs. Yet a single answer can depend on thousands of features interacting across many layers, and there is as yet no reliable way to tell whether an explanation captures everything that matters. For such reasons, the interpretability researcher Neel Nanda argues that interpretability will not reliably find deceptive AI on its own, though it can be one useful layer of defence among several (Nanda, 2025).

The evidence so far

Last checked 27 October 2026

Researchers can now find interpretable representations inside large commercial models. In 2024 Anthropic extracted millions of features from one of its production models, including features related to deception and sycophancy (Templeton et al., 2024), and showed that changing a feature changes behaviour: with a feature for the Golden Gate Bridge turned up, the model claimed to be the bridge (Anthropic, 2024). In 2025 Anthropic traced some of the internal steps by which a model reaches its answers, for example planning a rhyme before writing the line that leads to it (Anthropic, 2025).

Interpretability has begun to contribute to safety checks. Simple probes could tell almost perfectly when models deliberately trained to misbehave on a hidden cue (so-called sleeper agents) were about to behave badly, though it is unknown whether this would work for deception that arises naturally (Anthropic, 2024). Before releasing Claude Sonnet 4.5, Anthropic found that suppressing the model’s internal representation of being tested made misaligned behaviour more frequent in some tests (Anthropic, 2025).

Whether mechanistic methods do better than simpler ones is doubtful so far. Studies found that sparse autoencoders did not beat simpler methods at reading information from a model (Kantamneni et al., 2025) and that, for steering a model’s behaviour, simply prompting it worked better (Wu et al., 2025). When researchers tested methods for uncovering knowledge that models had been trained to hide, the most effective technique in most settings did not look inside the model at all (Cywiński et al., 2025).

What is established is that some concepts and reasoning steps inside large models can be found, read and changed, and that such findings have begun to inform safety checks. But mechanistic interpretability is still far from being able to reliably rule out dangerous goals in advanced AI, and whether it will do better than simpler methods is open.
